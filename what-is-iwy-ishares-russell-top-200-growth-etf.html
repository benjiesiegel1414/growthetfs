What Is $IWY? The iShares Russell Top 200 Growth ETF Explained (2026)

$IWY is the iShares Russell Top 200 Growth ETF: a $15.5 billion index fund that owns the growth stocks among the 200 largest U.S. companies. It charges 0.20%, holds about 100 stocks, and has returned 19.09% a year over the 10 years through September 30, 2026, per iShares. In plain English, it's a mega cap growth fund, and right now that means a big bet on $NVDA, $AAPL, $MSFT and Alphabet.

"What is IWY" is one of the most searched questions on this site right now, and I get why. The ticker is easy to confuse with $IWF, $IWL and $IWM, and iShares doesn't exactly market it hard. So here's what it actually owns, how it's done, what it costs, and how it stacks up against the funds people usually compare it to.

How $IWY works

Start with the Russell Top 200: the 200 biggest companies in the U.S. market. Russell then splits that group into growth and value using things like growth forecasts and sales growth. $IWY owns the growth side, weighted by market cap. Its sister fund $IWX owns the value side.

Because it starts with only the top 200 companies, $IWY skips the mid caps you'd get in a broader growth fund like $IWF (which tracks the Russell 1000 Growth). That's the whole point of it. You're getting the giants and nothing else.

This is the part most people miss: "growth ETF" and "mega cap growth ETF" sound similar, but the concentration is very different. In $IWY, the biggest companies don't just lead. They dominate.

A few things jump out:

  • $IWY has the best 10 year record of the group. $10,000 at 19.09% a year for 10 years grows to about $57,400. The same amount in $IWF at 17.94% gets you about $52,100.
  • The last year was rough for pure growth. $IWY made 8.09% while the S&P 500 growth fund $IVW made 18.21% and the blended $IWL made 15.50%. When the mega cap growth trade cools, $IWY feels it first.
  • The index is tracking well. Its benchmark returned 19.31% a year over 10 years versus 19.09% for the fund. That small gap is mostly the 0.20% fee.

$IWY vs $MGK vs $VUG

This is the comparison most people are really making. $MGK (Vanguard Morningstar Mega Cap Growth) is the closest twin: also a mega cap growth fund, with 56 stocks and about 67% in its top 10 per Vanguard's June 30, 2026 fact sheet. $VUG is broader at 147 stocks. I broke both down in my $VUG vs $VONG vs $MGK vs $VOOG comparison.

The big difference is cost. $MGK charges 0.05% and $VUG charges 0.03%. $IWY charges 0.20%. On $50,000, that's $100 a year for $IWY versus $25 for $MGK. Not life changing, but it's a headwind every single year for funds that own mostly the same top names.

Where $IWY can make sense: if you already use iShares, if your brokerage or 401(k) plan offers it commission free, or if you specifically want the Russell methodology. Otherwise, I'd at least look at the cheaper twins first. You can see each one on its own page: $MGK growth ETF page and $VUG growth ETF page.

Does $IWY pay a dividend?

Technically yes, quarterly. But it's tiny. The trailing 12 month yield was 0.33% as of September 30, 2026. The September 2026 payment was about $0.23 per share on a fund trading around $282. On $100,000 that's roughly $330 a year.

I'm a dividend investor first, so let me be blunt: nobody should buy $IWY for income. That's not its job. Its job is to compound. Where growth funds like this fit for a lot of income investors is the bucket you don't touch, the part of the portfolio that grows while your dividend ETFs cover the bills.

The risks

Concentration. 15% in one stock and about 61% in ten. If $NVDA has a bad year, $IWY has a bad year. There's no way around it.

Sector risk. Roughly 72% tech and communication. A tech selloff hits this fund harder than almost any broad index fund.

Valuation. The P/E ratio was 38.4 as of October 22, 2026. You're paying a premium for expected growth, and if that growth disappoints, prices can fall fast.

Volatility. iShares lists a 3 year beta of 1.18, so it has moved more than the market. The 5 year return (14.71% a year) includes the 2022 crash and is a more honest picture than the 3 year number.

Who $IWY is for

  • Long time horizon investors who want maximum exposure to the largest U.S. growth companies and can sit through big drawdowns.
  • People who already own a broad index fund and want to tilt harder toward mega cap growth.
  • Not for anyone who needs income, wants low volatility, or already owns $QQQ, $VUG or $MGK. You'd mostly be buying the same stocks again.

My takeaway

$IWY is a solid, well run mega cap growth index fund with a great 10 year record. It's also one of the most concentrated growth ETFs you can buy and it costs more than its Vanguard twins. If you want exactly this exposure, it does the job. If you just want "big growth stocks," check whether $MGK or $VUG gets you the same thing for less.

Either way, decide how much growth you want next to your income holdings first. That's the decision that actually matters. FAQ

What is $IWY?

$IWY is the iShares Russell Top 200 Growth ETF. It tracks the Russell Top 200 Growth Index, which holds the growth stocks among the 200 largest U.S. companies, weighted by market cap. It launched September 22, 2009 and had about $15.5 billion in net assets as of October 22, 2026.

What is $IWY's expense ratio?

0.20% a year, or $20 per $10,000 invested, per iShares. That's higher than Vanguard's mega cap growth fund $MGK (0.05%) and $VUG (0.03%).

What are $IWY's top holdings?

As of October 22, 2026, the top five were $NVDA (15.00%), $AAPL (8.63%), $MSFT (6.33%), $GOOGL (5.84%) and $AVGO (5.01%). The top 10 made up about 61% of the fund across 101 holdings.

What is the difference between $IWY and $IWF?

$IWY only holds growth stocks from the 200 largest U.S. companies. $IWF tracks the Russell 1000 Growth, so it also owns mid caps. Through September 30, 2026, $IWY returned 19.09% a year over 10 years versus 17.94% for $IWF. $IWY charges 0.20% and $IWF charges 0.18%.

Does $IWY pay dividends?

Yes, quarterly, but the yield is small: 0.33% on a trailing 12 month basis as of September 30, 2026. It's a growth fund, not an income fund.

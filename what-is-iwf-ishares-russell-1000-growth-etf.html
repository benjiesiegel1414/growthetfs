$IWF is the iShares Russell 1000 Growth ETF, a passive index fund that owns the growth half of the 1,000 largest U.S. companies. It's one of the biggest growth ETFs in the world, with about $117.8 billion in assets, 372 stocks and a 0.18% expense ratio. In plain English: it's the classic "large cap growth" benchmark in a fund, heavy on mega cap tech, with a long tail of mid caps.

"What is IWF," "IWF holdings" and "Russell 1000 growth ETF" keep showing up in the searches that bring people to GrowthETFs.com. So here's the full picture: how the index picks stocks, what the fund owns, how it's done, what it costs, why the share price suddenly looks a lot cheaper than it used to, and how it compares to $VONG, $IWY, $IUSG, $VUG and $SCHG.

How the Russell 1000 Growth Index picks stocks

$IWF doesn't have a manager picking "great growth companies." It follows an index from FTSE Russell, and knowing the rules tells you exactly what you're buying.

Step one is the universe. The Russell 1000 is roughly the 1,000 largest U.S. stocks. That covers large caps and the bigger mid caps. No small caps.

Step two is the style split. Each stock gets scored on value and growth traits. iShares describes the growth side as stocks selected using metrics such as growth forecasts and sales growth, while the value side leans on how cheap a stock is relative to its book value. Stocks that look clearly like growth go into the growth index. Stocks in the middle get split between growth and value.

Step three is weighting. Everything is weighted by market cap. Bigger company, bigger slice. That's why a few trillion dollar names dominate the fund, even with 372 stocks in it.

This is the part most people miss: because the Russell index and the S&P growth indexes use different rules, they don't own the same stocks in the same weights. That difference showed up in a big way over the last year, which I'll get to in the performance section.

What stands out to me:

It's top heavy. The top 10 add up to about 56% of the fund. The top three ($NVDA, $AAPL, $MSFT) are about 27%, and the two Alphabet share classes together are almost 10%. So yes, 372 stocks, but your result mostly depends on about ten of them.

Semis are a big theme. $NVDA, $AVGO and $AMD are all top 10, and Micron ($MU) is right behind at 2.85%, followed further down by Applied Materials and Lam Research. If chip stocks have a bad year, $IWF will feel it.

The names past the top 25 are small. Visa, Mastercard, Palantir, GE Aerospace, Caterpillar, Netflix and Home Depot all sit in the 0.7% to 2.3% range. The mid caps are in there, but individually they barely move the needle.

The 10 year number is excellent. 17.94% a year turns $10,000 into roughly $52,100 over a decade. That's the kind of compounding that makes growth funds worth owning in the accumulation years.

The 1 year number is the surprise. 7.29% looks weak next to the S&P growth funds. Over the same 12 months iShares reports 18.27% for $IUSG and 18.21% for $IVW. Same broad idea, "U.S. growth stocks," but an 11 point gap in one year, all because of the index rules and weights. It's a great reminder that "growth ETF" is not one thing. The index matters.

Recent stretch. iShares shows a NAV total return of -1.52% year to date through November 12, 2026, and a 52 week NAV range of $102.74 to $129.17. The three year beta is 1.25 and the three year standard deviation is 16.82%, so it swings harder than the market in both directions. Valuations are rich too: a P/E of 35.41 as of November 12, 2026.

The fee drag is visible. Over 10 years the fund trailed its index by about 0.21 points a year (17.94% vs 18.15%), which lines up with the 0.18% fee plus small trading costs. Keep that in mind for the $VONG comparison below.

Why is $IWF's price so much lower now?

If you remember $IWF trading at several hundred dollars a share, you're not imagining it. According to Digrin's split history, $IWF did a 4 for 1 share split on April 29, 2026. Every share became four shares at a quarter of the price, so the value of your position didn't change. It just made the fund easier to buy in round shares.

One practical note: when you look at old dividend amounts or old price charts, make sure they're split adjusted. Otherwise the numbers will look like the fund crashed or cut its dividend, and neither happened.

Who is $IWF for?

  • It might fit if you want the standard large cap growth benchmark in one liquid fund, with a little mid cap exposure riding along.
  • It might fit if you trade actively or use options, where $IWF's huge volume and tight spreads are a real advantage.
  • It probably isn't the cheapest way to own this exact index for a buy and hold account. $VONG holds the same index for a third of the fee.
  • It's not an income fund. About 0.4% yield.

Here's how I think about it. If I'm going to hold a growth fund for 10 or 20 years, I care about two things: that it owns what I expect, and that the fee is low. $IWF passes the first test easily. On the second, it's fine but not the best. If you're comparing it to other options, the full growth ETF list shows AUM and total return for every fund we track.

The risks

  • Concentration. About 56% in the top 10 and about 27% in the top three.
  • Tech dependence. 53% information technology, about 69% with communication services.
  • Valuation. A P/E around 35 means expectations are high.
  • Bigger swings. A three year beta of 1.25 per iShares.
  • Long dry spells. The since inception return of about 8.4% a year reflects a launch right before the 2000 to 2002 tech crash. Growth can look terrible for years.

The takeaway

$IWF is the iShares Russell 1000 Growth ETF: 372 large and mid cap U.S. growth stocks, about $118 billion in assets, a 0.18% fee, and a 10 year record of roughly 18% a year through September 2026. It's heavy in mega cap tech and semis, and it had a rough last 12 months compared to the S&P growth funds. It's a solid, liquid core growth holding, but if you're a buy and hold investor, look hard at $VONG, which tracks the same index for less. Read the prospectus before you buy.

FAQ

What is $IWF?

$IWF is the iShares Russell 1000 Growth ETF. It tracks the Russell 1000 Growth Index, the growth half of the 1,000 largest U.S. stocks, so it owns large and mid caps. It launched May 22, 2000, charges 0.18% a year and had about $117.8 billion in net assets as of November 13, 2026.

What are $IWF's top holdings?

As of November 12, 2026: NVIDIA (12.28%), Apple (8.81%), Microsoft (6.15%), Alphabet Class A (5.51%), Broadcom (5.37%), Alphabet Class C (4.45%), Tesla (3.68%), Eli Lilly (3.61%), Meta (3.55%) and AMD (3.02%). The top 10 were about 56% of the fund.

What is the difference between $IWF and $VONG?

They track the same index, the Russell 1000 Growth. $IWF charges 0.18% and has about $118 billion in assets. Vanguard's $VONG charges 0.06% and has about $45 billion. Same exposure, different price tag.

What is the difference between $IWF and $IWY?

$IWF owns growth stocks across the Russell 1000, including mid caps, and held 372 stocks as of November 12, 2026. $IWY only owns growth stocks among the 200 largest U.S. companies. $IWY charges 0.20% versus 0.18% for $IWF.

Does $IWF pay dividends?

Yes, quarterly, but it's tiny. The last four payments added up to about $0.43 per share, roughly 0.4% of the $114.89 price on November 13, 2026. The 30 day SEC yield was 0.30% as of October 31, 2026.

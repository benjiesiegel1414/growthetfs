Over the last 10 years, $VOOG has beaten $VOO: 17.45% a year versus 15.41%, which turned $10,000 into about $49,960 versus $41,940. But over the last 1 and 5 years, plain old $VOO came out ahead, per StockAnalysis data through October 30, 2026. $VOOG is the growth half of the S&P 500, so it wins big when tech leads and lags when it doesn't.

Searches like "VOO annualized return last 10 years", "VOO return rate", "VOO yearly returns" and "VOOG average annual return" keep showing up in this site's search data. They're really the same question: should I just own the whole S&P 500, or tilt toward the growth stocks inside it? So here's $VOO vs $VOOG side by side, with real numbers and no hype.

$VOO owns the whole S&P 500, weighted by size. $VOOG only owns the companies S&P scores as "growth" (things like sales growth, earnings growth and price momentum), which is about 157 stocks. Cut out the slower value names and what's left is a lot more top heavy. In $VOOG, Nvidia alone is 15.00% of the fund and Microsoft is 10.34%. In $VOO, they're 8.39% and 5.78%.

Here's what that looks like on $10,000:

  • Over 10 years: about $41,940 in $VOO versus $49,960 in $VOOG. That's an $8,000 gap from the growth tilt.
  • Over 5 years: about $17,660 in $VOO versus $17,290 in $VOOG. Basically a tie, with $VOO slightly ahead.

So if you're asking "what's VOO's annualized return over the last 10 years?", the answer is about 15.4% a year. That's well above the long run stock market average most people quote, because the last decade was an unusually strong run for big U.S. companies. I wouldn't plan my life around getting 15% forever.

Why $VOOG won the decade but lost the last 5 years

This is the part most people miss. The 10 year number and the 5 year number tell different stories, and both are true.

The 10 year window is dominated by the giant tech run. When Nvidia, Microsoft, Apple and friends are leading, a fund that owns more of them wins. $VOOG owns a lot more of them, so it pulled ahead.

The 5 year window starts in late 2021, right before growth stocks got crushed in 2022. $VOOG fell harder in that drawdown, and even after a big recovery, it's still roughly even with $VOO over that stretch. And over the last year, value and dividend stocks have had a strong run while some big tech names cooled off, so $VOOG trailed again.

That's the trade. A beta of 1.18 means $VOOG has tended to move about 18% more than the market. More upside when growth leads. More pain when it doesn't. If you want the full big picture on how growth funds work, I wrote it up in what is a growth ETF.

Fees and dividends: small numbers, real money

$VOO charges 0.03%. $VOOG charges 0.07%. On $100,000, that's $30 a year versus $70. Not a dealbreaker either way, but it's worth knowing.

On dividends, neither fund is an income play. $VOO yields about 1.07%, or roughly $1,070 a year on $100,000. $VOOG yields 0.48%, about $480. Both pay quarterly. As a dividend investor, I'd never buy either of these for the income. They're compounding tools. So which is better, $VOO or $VOOG?

Here's how I'd actually think about it:

  • $VOO if you want one simple core holding. You still own every growth stock in $VOOG, just at market weight, plus the value side for balance. Lower fee, lower volatility, a bit more income.
  • $VOOG if you're comfortable with bigger swings and want to lean into the companies growing fastest. It's been the better 10 year performer, but you need the stomach for years like 2022.
  • Both is what a lot of people end up with, but understand the overlap. Every stock in $VOOG is also in $VOO. Owning both is really just $VOO with an extra growth tilt.

I'd rather own the whole market as a base and add a tilt on purpose than chase whatever won last year. Growth won the decade. Value won the last 12 months. Nobody rings a bell when leadership flips.

The risks

  • Concentration. $VOOG's top 10 holdings are over 60% of the fund, and Nvidia alone is 15%. A rough patch for a few mega caps hits it hard.
  • Style risk. Growth can trail value for years. The last 5 years show it can trail even the plain S&P 500.
  • Recent returns aren't a forecast. A 15% to 17% a year decade is not normal. Plan on less.
  • Overlap. If you already own $VOO, $QQQ or a total market fund, adding $VOOG may mostly add more of the same tech names.

The takeaway

$VOOG beat $VOO over 10 years and since both launched in 2010, roughly 17.5% versus 15.4% a year over the decade. $VOO won the last 1 and 5 years, costs less, yields more and swings less. If you want a simple core, $VOO does the job. If you want a growth tilt and can handle the volatility, $VOOG has rewarded patient holders. Pick based on the ride you can actually sit through.

FAQ

What is $VOO's average annual return over the last 10 years?

About 15.41% a year with dividends reinvested, through October 30, 2026, per StockAnalysis. That turned $10,000 into roughly $41,940. Since its September 2010 launch, $VOO has returned about 14.64% a year.

What is $VOOG's average annual return?

About 17.45% a year over 10 years, 11.58% a year over 5 years and 16.34% a year since its September 2010 launch, all with dividends reinvested through October 30, 2026, per StockAnalysis.

Is $VOOG better than $VOO?

It depends on the period. $VOOG beat $VOO over 10 years (17.45% vs 15.41% a year), but $VOO won over 5 years (12.04% vs 11.58%) and the past year (11.21% vs 6.99%). $VOOG is more concentrated and more volatile. This is not a recommendation.

What is the difference between $VOO and $VOOG?

$VOO tracks the full S&P 500 with 518 holdings and a 0.03% fee. $VOOG tracks the S&P 500 Growth Index, about 157 of the faster growing S&P 500 companies, with a 0.07% fee. $VOOG's top 10 holdings are about 60% of the fund versus 39% for $VOO.

Does $VOOG pay a dividend?

Yes, quarterly, but it's small. $VOOG's trailing yield was 0.48% versus 1.07% for $VOO as of late October 2026, per StockAnalysis. Neither is built for income.

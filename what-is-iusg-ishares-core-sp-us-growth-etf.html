$IUSG is the iShares Core S&P U.S. Growth ETF, a passive index fund that owns the growth side of the S&P 900, which is the S&P 500 plus the S&P MidCap 400. It charges just 0.04% a year, holds 399 stocks and manages about $32.6 billion. In plain English: it's a cheap, broad U.S. growth fund that reaches a little further down into mid caps than most of its rivals.

"$IUSG," "iusg etf" and "iShares Core S&P U.S. Growth ETF" are searches that keep bringing people to GrowthETFs.com, so here's the whole picture: how the index picks stocks, what the fund actually owns, how it has done, what it costs, and how it stacks up against $IVW, $SPYG, $VOOG, $VUG and $SCHG.

How the S&P 900 Growth Index picks stocks

This is the part most people miss. $IUSG doesn't hand pick "growth companies." It follows a rules based index, and the rules are worth knowing because they explain some of the weird names you'll find inside.

Step one is the universe. The S&P 900 is the S&P 500 plus the S&P MidCap 400. So you get large caps and mid caps, but no small caps.

Step two is scoring. S&P scores every stock on three growth factors (three year earnings per share growth relative to price, three year sales per share growth, and 12 month price momentum) and three value factors (book to price, earnings to price and sales to price).

Step three is the split. Stocks that look clearly like growth go fully into the growth index. Stocks that look clearly like value go into the value index. The ones in the middle get split, with part of their market cap counted as growth and part as value. That's how a company like Berkshire Hathaway, which nobody calls a growth stock, can show up near the top of $IUSG.

iShares sums it up as tracking U.S. large and mid cap companies selected using metrics such as above average earnings and revenue growth. The index rebalances on a schedule, so the fund isn't trading every day based on someone's opinion. That's a feature if you want low turnover and low cost.

A few things jump out to me.

It's top heavy. The top 10 add up to about 56% of the fund, and the top three alone ($NVDA, $MSFT, $AAPL) are about 29%. Count both Alphabet share classes together and Alphabet is about 8.5%. So yes, 399 holdings, but the fund mostly moves with a few giants.

The mid caps are there, but small. The S&P 400 slice gives you hundreds of names you won't find in an S&P 500 growth fund, but each one is a tiny weight. Don't buy $IUSG expecting a mid cap fund. Think of it as a large cap growth fund with a thin mid cap tail.

The style split shows. Berkshire Hathaway in the top 10 is the clearest example of how S&P's split between growth and value works in practice.

Two takeaways.

It tracks its index almost perfectly. The gap to the index is a few hundredths of a point a year over the last decade. That's exactly what you want from a 0.04% index fund.

The long term number is a reminder. 17.6% a year over 10 years is a fantastic run. But the since inception figure is only about 8% a year, because the fund launched in July 2000, right before the dot com crash. Growth funds can go years looking bad. If you buy one, you need to be the kind of investor who can sit through that.

More recently, iShares shows a 9.59% NAV total return year to date through November 11, 2026, and a 52 week NAV range of $149.12 to $199.07. The fund's three year beta is 1.23 and its three year standard deviation is 16.23%, so it moves more than the overall market in both directions. It's also priced like growth: a P/E of 31.67 and a price to book of 8.60 as of November 12, 2026.

$IUSG vs $IVW. Same issuer, and this one surprises people. $IVW tracks only the S&P 500 growth slice and charges 0.18%. $IUSG covers the S&P 500 plus mid caps and charges 0.04%. If you're picking between the two iShares funds in a long term account, the fee difference alone is hard to ignore. On $100,000 that's about $140 a year, every year.

$IUSG vs $SPYG and $VOOG. Those two track the S&P 500 Growth index. $IUSG adds the mid cap growth names on top. In practice the returns will be close because the big names dominate all three, but $IUSG is a touch broader.

$IUSG vs $VUG and $SCHG. Different index families, slightly different rules for what counts as growth, and fewer holdings. $VUG and $SCHG are more concentrated at the top (StockAnalysis shows their top 10 at about 63% and 60%, versus about 56% for $IUSG). Who is $IUSG for?

  • It might fit if you want one cheap, broad U.S. growth fund and like the idea of a little mid cap exposure riding along.
  • It might fit if you want a 0.04% growth core that trades with tight spreads (iShares shows a 0.02% median bid/ask spread) at any major brokerage.
  • It probably doesn't fit if you already own an S&P 500 index fund and a Nasdaq-100 fund. You'd mostly be buying the same top names a third time.
  • It's not an income fund. About 0.5% yield, paid quarterly.

Here's how I think about it. In the growth ETF world, the cheap index funds are close substitutes. The differences between $IUSG, $SPYG, $VUG and $SCHG are real but small. What matters far more is that you pick one, keep the fee low, and actually hold it through the ugly years. If you're stuck between them, compare them on the full growth ETF list and pick the one you'll be comfortable owning for a decade.

The risks

  • Concentration. About 56% in the top 10 and about 29% in the top three.
  • Tech dependence. Roughly half the fund is information technology.
  • Valuation. A P/E around 32 means a lot of good news is already priced in.
  • Bigger swings. A three year beta of 1.23 per iShares. When the market drops, growth usually drops harder.
  • Long dry spells. The fund's since inception return of about 8% a year shows what a bad starting point (2000) can do.

The takeaway

$IUSG is iShares' low cost U.S. growth index fund: 399 large and mid cap stocks from the S&P 900 Growth Index, a 0.04% fee, and returns that have tracked the index almost to the decimal. It's heavy in mega cap tech and moves harder than the market, like every growth fund. If you want a cheap, slightly broader alternative to S&P 500 growth funds, it's a solid choice, and it's far cheaper than its sibling $IVW. Read the prospectus before you buy.

FAQ

What is $IUSG?

$IUSG is the iShares Core S&P U.S. Growth ETF. It tracks the S&P 900 Growth Index, the growth stocks of the S&P 500 and S&P MidCap 400. It launched July 24, 2000, charges 0.04% a year and held 399 stocks as of November 12, 2026.

What is the $IUSG expense ratio?

0.04% a year, per iShares. That's about $4 a year on $10,000, versus $18 for $IVW (0.18%).

What are $IUSG's top holdings?

As of November 11, 2026: NVIDIA (11.49%), Microsoft (10.58%), Apple (7.09%), Alphabet Class A (4.71%), Broadcom (4.49%), Meta (3.79%), Amazon (3.79%), Alphabet Class C (3.78%), Eli Lilly (3.14%) and Berkshire Hathaway (2.99%).

Does $IUSG pay dividends?

Yes, quarterly. The 12 month trailing yield was 0.50% as of October 31, 2026, and the most recent payment was $0.2445 per share on September 18, 2026.

What is the difference between $IUSG and $IVW?

Both are iShares growth funds. $IVW tracks the S&P 500 Growth Index and charges 0.18%. $IUSG tracks the S&P 900 Growth Index, which adds mid caps, and charges 0.04%.

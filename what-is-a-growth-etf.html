What Is a Growth ETF? How Growth ETFs Work, the Biggest Ones and What to Watch (2026)

A growth ETF is a fund that owns companies expected to grow their sales and earnings faster than the overall market, usually picked by an index that splits stocks into "growth" and "value." The biggest one, $VUG, holds about $225 billion, and the five largest large cap growth index ETFs on our list hold roughly $540 billion combined, per our GrowthETFs.com data. In plain English: you're buying the market's fastest growing companies in one ticker, and accepting more concentration and less income to get it.

"Growth ETF", "growth etfs", "growth equity ETF" and "what is a growth ETF" are some of the broadest searches that land on this site. So instead of another single fund breakdown, this is the big picture: how a stock gets the "growth" label, what these funds actually own, how they've done next to value funds, and what I'd look at before buying one.

How a stock becomes a "growth" stock

Nobody hand picks most growth ETFs. An index provider does it with a formula. It starts with a universe, like the S&P 500 or the Russell 1000, then scores every company on growth and value traits:

  • Growth traits: things like earnings growth, sales growth and forecasts of future growth. S&P's style indexes also look at price momentum.
  • Value traits: things like price to book, price to earnings and price to sales. Cheaper stocks score as value.

Companies that score high on growth go into the growth index. The cheaper, slower growing ones go into the value index. Some stocks get split between both. Then the fund weights what's left by market cap, which means the biggest companies get the biggest slice.

This is the part most people miss: every provider uses a slightly different formula. That's why two "large cap growth" ETFs can hold different stocks and post very different returns in the same year. More on that in a minute.

Over 10 years, these all landed within about 1.3 percentage points a year of each other. Over the last year, they were 11 points apart. $SPYG did 18.37% while $IWF did 7.29%, and both are "large cap growth ETFs."

Why? The S&P growth index uses its own screens and momentum, and it held up much better over the last 12 months than the Russell version. Over a long stretch the differences wash out a lot. Over a short stretch they can be huge. So don't buy a growth fund off one great year, and don't dump one after one bad year.

Two lessons jump out. First, over 10 years growth crushed value. $10,000 in $IWF at 17.94% a year grows to about $52,100. The same in $IWD at 11.24% grows to about $29,000. Second, the last year flipped the script completely: value did 23.59% while growth did 7.29%.

Styles go in and out of favor, sometimes for years at a time. And you pay for growth up front. A P/E of about 37 versus about 24 means investors are paying a lot more for every dollar of current earnings, betting those earnings keep growing fast. When they do, growth wins. The main types of growth ETFs

Large cap index growth funds

$VUG, $IWF, $SCHG, $SPYG, $IVW and $VONG. Cheap, huge and liquid. This is where most people should start. I compared Vanguard's four in my $VUG vs $VONG vs $MGK vs $VOOG breakdown.

Mega cap growth funds

$MGK and $IWY. Only the giants, and even more concentrated. What is $IWY? walks through one of them in detail.

Mid and small cap growth funds

$VOT, $IWP, $VBK and $IWO. Smaller, younger companies. More room to grow, more volatility, and they behave differently from the mega cap names that dominate the big funds.

Active growth funds

$JGRO, $FBCG, $CGGR and $TCHP. A manager picks the stocks instead of an index. Fees are higher, and the question is always whether they beat the cheap index funds after fees. I dug into that in are active growth ETFs worth it?

Sector and leveraged funds

Tech funds like $VGT and $FTEC, and leveraged funds like $TQQQ, show up on growth lists too. They're a different animal. A tech fund is one sector, not the whole growth market, and a 3x fund is a daily trading tool. Read my $TQQQ explainer before going anywhere near leverage.

Do growth ETFs pay dividends?

Most do, technically, but the checks are tiny. $IWY's trailing yield was 0.33% as of September 30, 2026, per iShares, and Vanguard put $VONG's yield at 0.4% at the end of June. On $100,000, that's a few hundred dollars a year. Compare that with the value side, where $IWD's trailing yield was 1.42% on the same date. Fast growing companies tend to reinvest their cash or buy back stock instead of paying big dividends.

I'm a dividend investor first, so let me be blunt: if you need your portfolio to pay your bills, a growth ETF won't do it on its own. That's what funds on TopDividendETFs.com are for. Where growth ETFs fit for a lot of income investors is the bucket you don't touch, the part that compounds while your dividend payers cover expenses. That's how I think about it: income to live on, growth to keep the whole thing ahead of inflation.

What I'd actually look at before buying one

  • The fee. Big index growth funds charge as little as 0.03% to 0.04%. Paying 0.18% for the same exposure is a choice, not a requirement.
  • The index. S&P, Russell, Morningstar and Dow Jones all define growth a little differently, and that drove an 11 point gap last year.
  • Concentration. In mid 2026, $NVDA alone was 12.6% to 13.8% of each of Vanguard's four growth funds, and 15% of $IWY in October. Know how much of your money sits in a handful of names.
  • Overlap. If you already own $VOO or $QQQ, a large cap growth fund adds a lot of the same stocks. Owning three growth ETFs often means owning one portfolio three times.
  • Total return, not price. Vanguard split $VUG 6 for 1 and $MGK 5 for 1 in April 2026, so old price charts are useless. Compare total returns.

The risks

  • Concentration. The biggest growth funds are top heavy, often with more than half the money in the top 10 stocks.
  • Sector risk. Growth funds lean hard into technology. A tech selloff hits them more than the broad market.
  • Valuation. You're paying a higher P/E for expected growth. If that growth disappoints, prices can drop fast.
  • Style cycles. Growth can lag value for long stretches, like the last 12 months, and you have to be able to sit through that.
  • Little income. Yields well under 1% mean you'd have to sell shares to fund spending.

My takeaway

A growth ETF is the simplest way to own the market's fastest growing companies in one trade. The big index funds are cheap, liquid and have done very well over the last decade, roughly 18% to 19% a year for the funds above. But "growth" is a label built by a formula, the funds are top heavy, and growth can trail value for long stretches. Pick a low cost fund, know which index it tracks, check what you already own, and treat it as the compounding part of your plan, not the income part.

FAQ

What is a growth ETF?

A growth ETF is a fund that owns companies expected to grow sales and earnings faster than the market, usually chosen by an index that sorts stocks into growth and value. The biggest, $VUG, held about $225 billion in late October 2026, per GrowthETFs.com data.

What is the largest growth ETF?

$VUG, the Vanguard Morningstar Growth ETF (formerly Vanguard Growth ETF), at about $225 billion in assets on October 29, 2026, per GrowthETFs.com data. $IWF, $IVW, $SCHG and $SPYG round out the five largest large cap growth index ETFs.

Is a growth ETF better than a value ETF?

It depends on the period. Through September 30, 2026, iShares reports its Russell 1000 Growth ETF ($IWF) returned 17.94% a year over 10 years versus 11.24% for its value twin ($IWD), but value won the last year, 23.59% versus 7.29%. Growth and value take turns leading.

Do growth ETFs pay dividends?

Most pay small dividends, usually well under 1% a year. $IWY's trailing yield was 0.33% as of September 30, 2026, per iShares. Growth ETFs are built for compounding, not income.

What should I look for in a growth ETF?

Start with the expense ratio, the index it tracks, how concentrated the top holdings are and how much it overlaps with funds you already own. Compare total returns rather than share prices. This is not a recommendation.
